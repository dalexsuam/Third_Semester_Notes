# <span style="color:rgb(223, 109, 109)">Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)</span>

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Definition</span>
Spinal cord stimulation is a **neuromodulation technique** that targets the **spinal neural circuits** by delivering controlled electrical stimulation through electrodes placed over the spinal cord. 

It can **modulate neural activity** and **promote neuroplastic changes**, enhancing motor and sensory functions after neurological injuries.

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>Approaches:</b></span>
1. **Epidural Spinal Cord Stimulation (eSCS)**
    - Involves the **surgical implantation** of electrodes over the **dura mater** (the membrane covering the spinal cord).
    - Allows **precise targeting** of spinal segments.
    - Considered **invasive**, but provides stable and localized stimulation.
        
2. **Transcutaneous Spinal Cord Stimulation (tSCS)**
    
    - **Non-invasive** approach using **surface electrodes** placed on the skin over the spine.
    - Easier to apply and safer, though less spatially selective.

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>Clinical Goals and Applications:</b></span>

- **Rehabilitation of neurologically impaired patients**, especially those with **spinal cord injuries (SCI)**.
- **Motor recovery and functional restoration**, such as improving **walking**, **cycling**, and **standing** abilities (motor bypassing).
- **Sensory enhancement**, including **tactile perception restoration**.
- **Reduction of spasticity**.
- **Pain management** — the only use **formally approved by CE marking and FDA devices** (for chronic, intractable pain).
- **Autonomic control**, including **cardiovascular regulation** and **bladder/sphincter control** in patients with SCI.
    

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>Conceptual Link:</b>  </span>
In a sense, spinal cord stimulation aims to achieve **the same type of neural activation and plasticity** as **peripheral functional electrical stimulation (FES)**, but it acts at a **different neural level** — directly on the **spinal networks** instead of the muscles.


## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">History of Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)</span>

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>1960s – Origins</b></span>
- The concept of **neuromodulation** began in the 1960s with the use of **invasive epidural spinal cord stimulation** to treat **chronic pain**.
- This development was largely **empirical**, based on clinical observations rather than theoretical understanding.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>1965 – Gate Control Theory (Melzack & Wall)</b></span>

- The **Gate Control Theory** proposed that stimulation of non-nociceptive afferents can **inhibit pain transmission**, acting as a _gatekeeper_ for nociceptive signals reaching the brain.
- This provided the first **theoretical framework** for how spinal stimulation could modulate pain perception.
    

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>1970s – Motor Effects</b></span>

- The first studies explored the **motor effects** of epidural stimulation, suggesting that SCS could potentially **influence motor control**, not just pain pathways.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>Late 1990s – Modeling and Non-Invasive Stimulation</b></span>

- Introduction of **transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS)** as a **non-invasive alternative** to epidural stimulation.
- Development of **computational models** (Holsheimer, 1998) to understand which spinal circuits were being activated, aiming to move beyond purely empirical approaches.
    

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>2000s – Neuroplasticity and Rehabilitation</b></span>

- Characterization of **motor reflexes** evoked by non-invasive stimulation.
- Growing evidence of **neuroplastic and rehabilitative effects** (Minassian 2007; Urbin 2017; Greiner 2021; De Freitas 2022).
- The field began shifting from **pain management** toward **functional recovery** in **spinal cord injury rehabilitation**.

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>Key Idea:</b>  </span>

Historically, **practice preceded theory** — clinicians observed beneficial effects before understanding the mechanisms.  
Today, **computational modeling** and **experimental studies** are helping bridge that gap, optimizing stimulation parameters for motor and sensory recovery.


## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Anatomy and Mechanism of Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112151249.png]]

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">- <b>Target area:</b>  </span>
    SCS targets the **neural centers of motor neurons** located in the spinal cord.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">- <b>Application site:</b>  </span>
    Whether **epidural** or **transcutaneous**, stimulation is always applied over the **dorsal (posterior) roots** of the spinal cord — _not the anterior part_ — because the posterior side is **more accessible**.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">- <b>Type of fibers stimulated:</b>  </span>
    The **posterior roots** contain **sensory afferent fibers**, whereas **motor efferent fibers** exit from the **anterior roots**.  
    → Therefore, SCS primarily **stimulates sensory afferents**, not motor neurons directly.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">- <b>Mechanism of action:</b>  </span>
    When the **sensory afferent fibers** are activated, they **trigger action potentials** that travel through **interneurons** within the spinal cord.  
    These **interneuronal circuits** then **excite motor neurons**, producing **motor output and muscle contraction** indirectly.
    
<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">- <b>Why sensory fibers are targeted:</b></span>

   - They have **lower activation thresholds** due to their **larger diameter** and **thicker myelin sheath**
   - They are located **posteriorly**, where stimulation electrodes are positioned.

<span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)"><b>In short:</b>  </span>
SCS doesn’t activate motor neurons directly.  
It **stimulates sensory afferents** in the dorsal roots, which then **indirectly excite motor neurons** through **interneuronal connections**, resulting in controlled muscle activation.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">PRM reflex and Motor facilitation</span>


![[Pasted image 20251112152435.png]]
When we apply **spinal cord stimulation (SCS)**, the physiological mechanism we rely on is called the **Posterior Root Muscle reflex**, or **PRM reflex**. This reflex represents the motor response that follows the activation of **sensory afferent fibers** in the **posterior (dorsal) roots** of the spinal cord. Regardless of whether the stimulation is **epidural** or **transcutaneous**, the electrode placement always targets the **posterior region**, where sensory fibers are located. These fibers are easier to excite because of their large diameter and thick myelin sheath, which gives them a lower activation threshold compared to motor efferents.

When an electrical pulse is delivered, it travels along these sensory afferent fibers and reaches the spinal cord, where it excites a network of **interneurons**. These interneurons then **relay the excitation** to **motor efferent neurons** located in the anterior horn of the spinal cord. As a result, the motor fibers fire and produce a **short-latency muscle contraction**. This chain of events—starting from sensory activation and ending with motor output—constitutes the **PRM reflex**. In practical terms, this reflex appears as a single spike or peak in the EMG signal that follows each individual stimulation pulse.

![[Pasted image 20251112152533.png]]

However, even though PRM reflexes clearly demonstrate that spinal circuits remain excitable and functional below the site of injury, they occur as **isolated responses**. That means that each stimulation pulse produces only one brief, independent contraction. By themselves, these reflexes are not sufficient to produce coordinated or sustained movements, which are necessary for functional rehabilitation. To move from simple reflex activation toward meaningful motion, stimulation must be delivered in a **repetitive and controlled manner**, taking into account the spinal network’s intrinsic dynamics.

![[Pasted image 20251112152605.png]]

One important phenomenon that influences how the spinal network behaves under repeated stimulation is known as **Post-Activation Depression**, or **PAD**. This effect can be observed when two or more electrical pulses are delivered in quick succession. For example, if two pulses are spaced very close together—say, about 50 milliseconds apart—the first pulse will evoke a clear EMG response, but the second one will produce a much smaller response, or sometimes almost none at all. As the interval between pulses becomes longer, the amplitude of the second response gradually recovers to the same level as the first.

This reduction in the second response is not caused by the **refractory period of the motor fibers**, which would occur if the motor neurons themselves were directly stimulated. Instead, it is due to the **synaptic dynamics** within the spinal cord—the **interneurons** that connect the sensory and motor pathways temporarily lose some of their excitability right after transmitting the first signal. This is why the second pulse produces a weaker reflex: the interneuronal network has not yet fully recovered from the first activation.

In other words, **PAD is a form of short-term synaptic fatigue** that occurs in the spinal circuitry involved in the PRM reflex. It reflects the temporal limits of the spinal network’s capacity to process repeated stimuli. Because of this, continuous high-frequency stimulation can lead to diminished reflex responses, making it difficult to achieve stable or powerful contractions through simple repetition of single pulses.

Understanding both the **PRM reflex** and **post-activation depression** is essential for designing effective spinal stimulation strategies. The goal is not only to evoke movement but to do so in a way that the spinal circuits can sustain coordinated activity. Therefore, researchers and clinicians work to identify stimulation parameters—such as frequency, intensity, and timing—that can maximize functional activation while minimizing the effects of post-activation depression.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">PRM Reflex vs. H-Reflex</span>

Although both the **PRM reflex** and the **H-reflex** involve sensory afferent activation leading to motor output, their mechanisms and locations are fundamentally different.

The **H-reflex** is elicited by stimulating a **peripheral mixed nerve** (for example, the tibial nerve). The electrical stimulus preferentially activates **Ia afferent fibers**, which carry the signal to the spinal cord, where it directly excites **α-motor neurons** via a **monosynaptic connection**. This results in a measurable muscle response — the **H-wave** — in the EMG signal. Because it is peripheral and monosynaptic, the H-reflex provides a **clean and focused** way to assess the integrity of the reflex arc and motor neuron excitability.

In contrast, the **PRM reflex** arises from **stimulation at the spinal level**, specifically targeting the **posterior roots** that contain the **sensory afferent fibers**. This kind of stimulation, used in **spinal cord stimulation (SCS)**, activates a broader network of interneurons inside the spinal cord, which then excite motor neurons **indirectly**. The resulting EMG response appears as **isolated single-pulse activations** rather than continuous rhythmic activity. Because this stimulation is **less localized** and **not monosynaptic**, it reflects the activation of complex spinal circuits rather than a simple reflex arc.

In summary, the **H-reflex** is a **peripheral, monosynaptic, and highly specific** reflex used for diagnostic purposes, whereas the **PRM reflex** is a **central, polysynaptic spinal reflex** that forms the physiological basis for **spinal cord stimulation–induced muscle activation**.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">PRM Reflex vs. H-Reflex — Latency and PAD Comparison<br></span>


The **latency difference** between the PRM and H-reflex comes from the **site of stimulation**.  
In the **PRM reflex**, the stimulation is applied directly at the **posterior roots** of the spinal cord, so the **afferent sensory fibers** are depolarized very close to the spinal circuits that connect to the motor neurons. Because the pathway is so short, the motor response appears **earlier on the EMG**, showing **shorter latency**.

In contrast, the **H-reflex** originates from the **peripheral nerve**, meaning the sensory impulse has to travel **all the way up to the spinal cord**, synapse with the motor neuron, and then the motor signal must travel **back down to the muscle**. This longer conduction path results in **longer latency** in the EMG response.

Both reflexes exhibit **post-activation depression (PAD)** — a reduction in the amplitude of the second EMG response when two stimuli are delivered in close succession. In both cases, this phenomenon is mediated by **Ia afferent fibers** from muscle spindles. PAD doesn’t arise from the refractory period of the motor fibers but rather from **synaptic and interneuronal dynamics**, where the connections involved in transmitting the reflex temporarily become less excitable after the first activation.

In essence:

- **PRM → faster, spinal-level reflex** (short loop)
- **H-reflex → slower, peripheral-level reflex** (long loop)
- **Both → show PAD due to Ia afferent-mediated synaptic fatigue**

| **Latency**                          | **Shorter latencies**, because the depolarization occurs **proximally**, at the level of the **posterior roots of the spinal cord**. The sensory fibers are activated very close to the motor neurons. | **Longer latencies**, because the depolarization occurs **distally**, at the **peripheral nerve**. The signal must travel to the spinal cord before eliciting a motor response. |

| **Post-Activation Depression (PAD)** | Present — mediated by **Ia afferent fibers** from muscle spindles. The second response is reduced if stimuli are close together.                                                                       | Also present — mediated by **Ia afferent fibers** from muscle spindles. The same mechanism causes a smaller second response when stimuli are too close in time.                 |
| **Functional Meaning**               | Reflects **spinal-level modulation** of reflex excitability; PAD highlights the **synaptic fatigue** of interneuronal circuits within the spinal cord.                                                 | Reflects **peripheral-level modulation**; PAD demonstrates **presynaptic inhibition** and synaptic depression within the monosynaptic reflex arc.                               |

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Transcutaneous vs. Epidural Spinal Cord Stimulation</span>

Up to this point, we have discussed **spinal cord stimulation (SCS)** in general terms, without distinguishing _how_ it is applied. Now we can specify that there are **two main approaches** to deliver spinal cord stimulation:  
**Transcutaneous SCS (tSCS)** and **Epidural SCS (eSCS)**.

![[Pasted image 20251112170946.png]]

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Transcutaneous Spinal Cord Stimulation (tSCS)</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112171014.png]]
This is a **non-invasive neuromodulation technique**.  
In this case, the electrodes are placed **on the skin**, typically over the **paravertebral** and **abdominal regions**, to deliver electrical currents that stimulate the spinal circuitry through the skin and underlying tissues. The stimulation generates a **current flow** that indirectly reaches the **posterior roots** of the spinal cord.

However, because the current must pass through several layers of tissue — skin, fat, and muscle — the **tissue conductivity** has a strong influence on how effectively the stimulation reaches the target. This may lead to **involuntary contractions** of nearby muscles, such as those along the spine or the abdominal wall, which can sometimes be uncomfortable for the subject.

Despite these limitations, **tSCS** has several important advantages:

- It is **non-invasive** and therefore **safe**.
- It is **easy to apply** and **low-cost**, requiring no surgery.
- It is **ideal for temporary or short-term treatments** and for **experimental or rehabilitation settings**.

The main disadvantages are that the stimulation is **less selective**, since the current must be stronger to penetrate the tissues, and therefore it tends to activate a **larger portion of the posterior roots**. This makes it more difficult to focus on a specific spinal segment.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Epidural Spinal Cord Stimulation (eSCS)</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112171039.png]]

Epidural SCS, on the other hand, is an **invasive neuromodulation technique**.  
In this case, **electrodes are surgically implanted** in the **epidural space**, directly over the **posterior roots** of the spinal cord. The electrical current is delivered either through **percutaneous leads** connected to an external stimulator, or through a **fully implanted device**.

Because the electrodes are located much closer to the target, the current can be much more **focused and selective**. In fact, around **80–90% of the current** flows within the **highly conductive cerebrospinal fluid**, which ensures that the stimulation directly affects the posterior roots with high precision.

This higher selectivity allows **epidural stimulation** to effectively activate **specific spinal circuits**, producing controlled and reproducible responses. However, the main issue with this technique is **long-term stability**.

Over time, the body reacts to the implanted electrodes by forming a **layer of connective tissue** around them — a natural immune response meant to isolate foreign materials. This reaction can **increase the electrical impedance** and **reduce the efficacy** of stimulation.

To address this, researchers have explored two main strategies:

1. **Developing biomimetic electrode materials**, designed to be better accepted by the body and reduce the foreign-body reaction.
2. **Reducing the immune response** by applying **brief stimulation protocols** or designing **less reactive electrode surfaces**.

###  <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Summary of Key Differences</span>

|**Feature**|**Transcutaneous SCS (tSCS)**|**Epidural SCS (eSCS)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Type**|Non-invasive neuromodulation|Invasive neuromodulation|
|**Electrode placement**|On the paravertebral and abdominal skin|Implanted on the posterior roots, in the epidural space|
|**Current path**|Passes through skin, fat, and muscle|Conducted through cerebrospinal fluid (80–90%)|
|**Selectivity**|Low (broad activation)|High (focused activation)|
|**Safety**|Very safe and accessible|Surgical risks; requires monitoring|
|**Cost**|Cheap|Expensive|
|**Application duration**|Temporary|Long-term or chronic applications|
|**Typical side effects**|Paraspinal or abdominal muscle contractions|Possible loss of efficacy due to tissue encapsulation|
|**Main limitation**|Low selectivity and indirect stimulation|Long-term electrode stability|

In summary, **tSCS** is an excellent **non-invasive and accessible option**, particularly useful for **rehabilitation and research contexts**, while **eSCS** offers **precise and sustained activation** of spinal circuits, making it more suitable for **long-term therapeutic interventions**, though at the cost of surgical complexity and potential stability issues.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Comparison Between Transcutaneous and Epidural SCS: Similarities and Mechanisms of Action</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112171656.png]]

When we compare **transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS)** and **epidural spinal cord stimulation (eSCS)**, an interesting result emerges: **the physiological responses they produce are remarkably similar**.

In fact, several studies — including _Hofstoetter et al., 2019_ — have demonstrated that both stimulation methods are capable of eliciting **very similar Posterior Root Muscle (PRM) reflexes**. In both cases, **post-activation depression (PAD)**, which we discussed before, is also observed. Moreover, both techniques can generate **comparable bi- or tri-phasic EMG waveforms**, and **no statistically significant differences** are found in their **onset latencies**.

This means that although one method is **non-invasive** (tSCS) and the other is **invasive** (eSCS), the **final functional outcome in terms of spinal activation** can be quite similar. So, the difference between them lies mainly in the **application modality**, **selectivity**, and **practical aspects**, rather than in the **type of physiological response** that they can produce.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Mechanism Behind Spinal Stimulation and Motor Facilitation<br></span>
![[Pasted image 20251112171809.png]]

Now, how exactly does spinal cord stimulation facilitate motor function?

There are several interpretations, but one of the **most accepted hypotheses** is that **SCS acts by modulating the excitability of spinal networks** — in other words, it changes the **state of readiness** of the neurons in the spinal cord.

Let’s imagine the spinal cord of a person with a spinal cord injury.  
Even if the main motor pathways are partially damaged, the spinal cord below the lesion is not completely silent. There are still **tiny subthreshold signals**, weak electrical “whispers,” traveling along the remaining neural fibers. These signals are **too weak to trigger a motor response**, because they do not reach the **activation threshold** of the motor neurons.

When we apply **spinal cord stimulation**, even with an **under-threshold stimulus**, it produces a kind of **tonic depolarization** in the region. This shifts the **central excitability state** of the spinal neurons **closer to their firing threshold**.

As a consequence, those weak “whispering” signals coming from **supraspinal regions** — the parts of the spinal cord or brain that are still functional — can now **reach the threshold** and **produce an effective motor output**. In other words, SCS does not create movement by itself, but rather **enables the residual voluntary signals** to become effective enough to activate the motor neurons and trigger muscle contractions.

This mechanism reflects a **neuro-modulatory effect**, rather than a purely stimulatory one:  
SCS enhances the **responsiveness** of spinal networks, making them more sensitive to the remaining descending inputs.

---

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Clinical Implications and the ASIA Scale</span>

This hypothesis also fits very well with clinical observations in **spinal cord injury (SCI) rehabilitation**.

Clinicians use the **ASIA scale (American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale)** to classify the severity of spinal cord lesions:

- **ASIA A**: complete injury — no motor or sensory function below the lesion level.
- **ASIA B/C/D**: incomplete injuries — some degree of preserved sensory or motor activity.

What is interesting is that **MRI imaging often shows that even in apparently “complete” lesions**, some **small neural fibers remain intact**, capable of transmitting weak signals across the lesion site.

These remaining fibers may not produce visible movement on their own, but they can **carry minimal electrical activity** — and this is precisely where spinal cord stimulation can play a key role.

By **increasing the excitability** of spinal circuits, SCS can **amplify these weak signals** and allow them to generate a measurable motor output. So, the presence of even a small number of preserved pathways is enough to make **motor facilitation through SCS** possible.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Activation of Central Pattern Generators (CPGs)</span>

The second major hypothesis regarding how **spinal cord stimulation (SCS)** facilitates motor function is still within the framework of **spinal neuromodulation**, but it focuses on a **circuit-based mechanism** rather than a purely **electrical excitability shift**.

In this view, SCS acts by **reactivating intrinsic spinal circuits** known as **Central Pattern Generators (CPGs)**.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">What Are CPGs?</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112172522.png]]
**Central Pattern Generators** are specialized networks of **interneurons located in the spinal cord** that are capable of generating **rhythmic, coordinated motor patterns** — such as **walking, stepping, or arm swinging** — even **without any input from the brain or sensory feedback from the body**.

In healthy conditions, these circuits operate under the influence of **descending signals from supraspinal centers** and **afferent sensory feedback**, ensuring smooth, synchronized motion. For example, CPGs are responsible for **automatic weight shifts between the legs** and the **oscillation of the arms** that naturally accompany locomotion (_Zehr et al., 2016_).

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">What Happens After a Spinal Cord Lesion?</span>

After a **spinal cord injury**, the **communication between the brain and the spinal CPGs** can be **partially or completely interrupted**. This means that the spinal circuits below the lesion are still present and potentially functional, but **they are disconnected from their supraspinal control**.

As a result, the **CPGs remain inactive**, not because they are damaged, but because **they lack sufficient input** — the descending commands or afferent triggers that normally start and coordinate their rhythmic activity.

However, the **spinal tissue below the lesion** is often **structurally intact and physiologically healthy**, which means that **these networks can still be engaged** if they receive the right kind of stimulation.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">How SCS Reactivates the CPGs</span>

When **spinal cord stimulation** is applied, especially through **posterior root activation** (which targets sensory afferents entering the spinal cord), it can **provide the missing input** that re-engages these dormant CPGs.

In other words, **SCS substitutes for the lost descending commands**, supplying an **artificial rhythmic input** that drives the spinal networks responsible for locomotor patterns.

This mechanism is particularly evident when stimulation is delivered at **frequencies between 20 and 50 Hz**, which has been shown to produce:

- **Locomotor-like EMG activity**,
- **Alternating leg flexion and extension movements**, and
- **Increased activation during cyclic movement tasks**.

Even in individuals lying supine (not standing or walking), **SCS at these frequencies can evoke rhythmic, stepping-like movements**, showing that **the spinal networks for locomotion can be activated independently of volitional control**.

## <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Two Complementary Hypotheses</span>

To summarize, we now have **two main interpretations** of how SCS promotes motor facilitation:

| **Aspect**        | **Excitability Shift Hypothesis**                                                                                         | **Central Pattern Generator Hypothesis**                                                               |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Focus**         | Electrical modulation                                                                                                     | Neural circuit reactivation                                                                            |
| **Mechanism**     | Subthreshold stimulation increases the excitability of spinal neurons, making residual descending signals more effective. | Stimulation reactivates intrinsic spinal circuits (CPGs) responsible for rhythmic, synergic movements. |
| **Key Concept**   | Brings spinal neurons closer to firing threshold.                                                                         | Triggers rhythmic motor patterns even without supraspinal input.                                       |
| **Effect**        | Facilitates voluntary movement transmission across incomplete lesions.                                                    | Produces automatic, rhythmic stepping-like movements.                                                  |
| **Type of Input** | Under-threshold tonic input modulating local excitability.                                                                | Sensory afferent input activating network-level coordination.                                          |

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) vs. Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES)</span>

Although both **Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)** and **Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES)** use electrical currents to restore or enhance motor function, **their mechanisms, targets, and goals are fundamentally different**.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)</span>

**SCS** works by **modulating the excitability of spinal circuits**, rather than directly causing muscles to contract.  It does not “drive” movement, but instead **facilitates** it — it **opens the gate** for whatever residual voluntary or reflexive control still exists in the patient’s nervous system.

In practice, this means that **SCS enhances the transmission of signals** from **supraspinal (brain)** and **propriospinal** pathways to **spinal motor neurons**.

So, if after a spinal cord injury there are still **some preserved fibers** — even if weak or insufficient to trigger a movement on their own — spinal stimulation can **increase their effectiveness**. It makes the spinal cord more “receptive” to those weak descending signals, allowing the person to **voluntarily initiate or modulate** movements that would otherwise be impossible.

In short:

> 🔹 **SCS does not directly generate movement — it facilitates it.**  
> 🔹 It acts as a **neuromodulatory tool** that enhances the body’s **natural motor pathways**, increasing the probability that volitional signals from the brain successfully activate motor neurons.

This is why we often say that **SCS is cooperative**: movement happens through a **co-adaptation** between the subject’s residual abilities and the artificial input. The stimulation provides the physiological conditions for movement, but the **subject’s intention and learning** remain essential.

Clinically, this leads to **real but partial motor recovery** — the ability to **stand, step, or perform assisted walking**— and most importantly, an increase in **autonomy and quality of life**. Patients often report being able to stand up to reach objects, communicate at eye level, or perform small tasks independently — all of which have profound psychological and social value, even if walking itself remains limited.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES)</span>

By contrast, **FES** directly **activates the peripheral motor nerves or muscles**.  
It is an **output-driven** approach: electrical stimulation is applied to the **nerves or muscles themselves**, causing **immediate contraction** and thus **producing movement** — even in the complete absence of voluntary control.

In FES, the goal is to **replace the lost neural drive** by artificially generating the muscle activation pattern that would normally come from the brain and spinal cord.

For example, electrodes are placed on or near the muscles of the leg, arm, or hand, and controlled electrical pulses are sent in a **timed, coordinated manner** to produce functional tasks — such as grasping, stepping, or cycling.

So:

> 🔹 **FES directly generates movement.**  
> 🔹 The electrical current causes muscle contractions that mimic natural movement patterns.

Over time, the **repetition of these artificial movements** can help **retrain the brain**, contributing to neuroplasticity and potentially improving voluntary motor control. However, during the stimulation itself, the **movement is completely induced by the system**, not by the patient’s voluntary intention.


###  <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Summary of the Core Differences</span>

|**Aspect**|**Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)**|**Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Target**|Spinal circuits and interneurons (posterior roots, spinal networks).|Peripheral motor nerves and muscles.|
|**Mechanism**|Modulates excitability of spinal networks, enhancing transmission of residual voluntary signals.|Directly depolarizes motor neurons or muscles to produce contraction.|
|**Goal**|Facilitate natural motor pathways; enable volitional control.|Substitute lost motor control; generate artificial movement.|
|**Nature of Movement**|Indirect — movement arises from patient’s own effort aided by neuromodulation.|Direct — movement is caused by external stimulation pattern.|
|**Therapeutic Role**|Enhances coordination between brain, spine, and muscles; supports neuroplasticity and voluntary recovery.|Produces functional motion to assist or replace lost motor output; promotes relearning through repetition.|
|**Invasiveness**|Can be transcutaneous (non-invasive) or epidural (invasive).|Typically non-invasive (surface electrodes) or minimally invasive (implanted electrodes).|
|**Example Use**|Standing and stepping in paraplegic subjects through spinal reactivation.|Hand grasp, foot drop correction, or cycling for paralyzed limbs.|

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Interaction and Co-adaptation</span>

An important concept that your professor emphasized is that **SCS works 

through co-adaptation:  It’s not the stimulation alone that produces the function, but the **interaction between the stimulation, the subject’s residual capacity, and their learning**.

This co-adaptation reflects the **plasticity of the nervous system**, where both the **human and the artificial system “learn” together** — the subject adjusts posture, timing, and intention, while the stimulation optimizes the excitability of the spinal networks.

Meanwhile, in **FES**, the adaptation is mostly at the **brain level**: repeated artificial activation of muscles helps the brain **relearn** or **reinforce motor patterns**, but the movement during stimulation is mechanically imposed.


### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">In Simple Terms</span>

- **SCS:** “I help your nervous system work better.”
- **FES:** “I move your muscles for you.”
    

SCS **facilitates** movement through **neural modulation**, while FES **produces** movement through **muscle activation**.


## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Understanding the Long-Term Effects and Open Questions in Spinal Cord Stimulation<br></span>

Although **spinal cord stimulation (SCS)** has shown impressive potential in restoring movement and function in neurologically impaired individuals, we are still far from fully understanding _why_ and _how_ these effects occur. The current scientific consensus is that **SCS can enhance the excitability and connectivity of spinal circuits**, producing **positive long-term changes** in the nervous system.

These long-term effects are believed to result from **strengthening the remaining neural pathways** between the spinal cord and the cortex, as well as **promoting plasticity** in spinal inhibitory circuits. In practical terms, repeated SCS may reinforce the communication between surviving neurons, allowing them to reorganize and form new functional connections. Some studies have even shown that **benefits persist after the stimulation ends**, which suggests a form of **Hebbian learning** — the principle that “neurons that fire together, wire together.” When electrical stimulation coincides with the subject’s voluntary effort, it enhances neuroplasticity, facilitating the recovery of motor control.

However, despite these promising findings, **we still don’t have a definitive explanation** of the underlying mechanisms. There are two major hypotheses:

- One views SCS as a **form of explicit learning** by the subject — where repeated co-activation of the spinal and cortical pathways strengthens control through experience and feedback.
- The other considers it a **true neuromodulatory phenomenon**, where the stimulation itself reshapes the functional state of the spinal networks, even without conscious participation.
    


At present, we can’t say with certainty which mechanism predominates — and most likely, both processes coexist to some degree. Moreover, since the **clinical use of SCS implants is relatively recent**, the **very long-term outcomes** (over years or decades) remain largely unknown.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">The Need for Standardization in SCS Research</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112175240.png]]

One of the biggest challenges today is that **SCS is not yet a standardized procedure** — not in protocol, not in reporting, and not even in terminology. As your professor pointed out, there are numerous ongoing experiments worldwide — in the United States, Switzerland, Italy, and other countries — each using slightly different setups, stimulation parameters, and assessment methods.

For example, a study might report excellent results, but fail to specify essential details such as **pulse duration, carrier frequency, posture of the subject**, or whether the stimulation was **monophasic or biphasic**. Then, when another research group tries to reproduce the same experiment, the results may vary drastically — even due to something as simple as a **10-degree difference in trunk position**, which changes the coupling between the electrodes and the spinal circuits.

This lack of uniformity is problematic because **scientific progress relies on reproducibility**. Without consistent reporting, even significant discoveries lose reliability and cannot be effectively compared or replicated. Recognizing this issue, a large research consortium recently published a **standardized framework for documenting spinal cord stimulation experiments** — the work by **Malik et al., 2024**.

Their guidelines define the **minimum parameters that must be reported** in any SCS study, including:

![[Pasted image 20251112175313.png]]
- **Pulse train frequency**, typically between **5 and 30 Hz**,
- **Carrier frequency**, usually in the range of **2.5 to 10 kHz**,
- **Current amplitude**, which can vary from **10–20 mA up to 100–200 mA**, depending on tolerance,
- **Pulse width**, generally **1–2 ms**,
- **Waveform type**, whether **monophasic** or **biphasic**,
- And importantly, **the subject’s posture and position during stimulation**, since even minor changes can alter the current pathways within the body.
    

They also introduced the concept of **Resting Motor Threshold (RMT)** — the minimum current necessary to elicit a motor response. In experimental protocols, over-threshold currents are used to identify and analyze the **Posterior Root Muscle (PRM) reflex**, whereas **under-threshold or threshold-level currents** are applied for continuous stimulation, aiming for sustained modulation without discomfort.

Furthermore, they highlighted that **monophasic and biphasic stimulations** can produce distinct physiological effects: monophasic waves tend to **enhance muscle strength**, while biphasic ones are thought to **promote finer motor control** by affecting different populations of neurons depending on the electrode polarity.

![[Pasted image 20251112175419.png]]

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">In Summary</span>

In essence, the field of spinal cord stimulation is still **in a state of evolution**. We know it can **modulate spinal circuits**, **promote recovery**, and **induce plasticity**, but we are still discovering _how_ and _under what conditions_ these effects are maximized.

The ongoing development of **reporting standards** marks a critical step toward building a consistent scientific foundation. By ensuring that every researcher “speaks the same language” when documenting their experiments, we will be able to compare results, identify best practices, and ultimately translate this promising technology into **reliable, reproducible clinical therapies**.

So, what you learned today represents **a snapshot of a rapidly progressing field** — one where both the science and the methodology are still being written.


## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Transcutaneous Spinal Cord Stimulation (tSCS): Electrode Placement, Hardware, and Emerging Applications</span>

Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation, or **tSCS**, is a **non-invasive alternative** to implanted spinal stimulators. Instead of surgically placing electrodes near the spinal cord, surface electrodes are positioned on the **skin over the spine** to deliver electrical currents capable of modulating the underlying neural structures.
![[Pasted image 20251112201555.png]]

Typically, electrodes are arranged in a **bipolar configuration**, commonly over spinal segments such as **L1–L2** or **T11–T12**. This setup helps generate a **more localized electric field**, allowing the stimulation to affect specific spinal segments with better selectivity. The electrodes themselves are usually **superficial, rectangular or square**, and can vary in size from **5 to 10 centimeters**. However, there is still significant variability among studies regarding the **exact size, shape, and position** of the electrodes — whether they are placed **symmetrically on both sides** of the spine or **asymmetrically**, such as across the abdomen and posterior back.

This lack of consensus is an important open issue in the field. Different electrode placements can change the distribution of the electric field and, consequently, the comfort and effectiveness of the stimulation. Some studies report **discomfort or sensory burden** during stimulation, while others achieve better tolerability depending on the **electrode material, contact area, or current density**. For this reason, one of the critical elements proposed by Malik et al. (2024) in their **standard reporting framework** is the **explicit description of the stimulation hardware** — including electrode characteristics and positioning — to make experiments reproducible and comparable across laboratories.

###  <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">SCS Hardware Reporting</span>
![[Pasted image 20251112201611.png]]
The **hardware description** is now considered a mandatory part of SCS documentation. Researchers must report not only the type of **stimulator device** used (for example, its output capabilities, pulse waveform, and compliance voltage) but also detailed information about **electrode geometry, surface area, and contact medium** (such as gel or saline). These parameters determine how the current distributes through the tissues, how deep the electric field penetrates, and how selectively spinal networks are activated.

Such standardization is essential because even small variations in electrode design or placement can lead to large differences in **neural recruitment**, **stimulation comfort**, and **therapeutic outcomes**.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Expanding Applications: Cervical tSCS</span>

While much of the early research on spinal cord stimulation focused on the **lumbosacral region** for gait rehabilitation, more recent studies have begun exploring **cervical-level stimulation** aimed at restoring **upper-limb function**. Cervical tSCS targets the **C5–T1 spinal roots**, which are responsible for arm and hand control.

However, **cervical stimulation is substantially more complex** than lumbosacral stimulation. This is because upper-limb movements are **not rhythmic or cyclic** like walking or cycling. Instead, they involve a wide variety of tasks — reaching, grasping, pushing, or even performing bilateral movements — each requiring different patterns of muscle activation. The challenge, therefore, lies in designing stimulation strategies that can adapt to these variable motor demands while maintaining postural stability and comfort.

Despite the difficulty, **initial clinical evidence is promising**. Studies have shown that **cervical tSCS can enhance hand strength, dexterity, and overall motor control** in patients with spinal cord injuries. Even more remarkably, some of these improvements have been **maintained for up to six months after the stimulation protocol ended**, suggesting that **lasting neuroplastic changes** were induced within the central nervous system.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Coupling Between Spinal Networks</span>

An especially interesting area of research involves **spinal network coupling**, which refers to the **bidirectional communication between the cervical and lumbar spinal segments**. Engaging both regions simultaneously through targeted tSCS has been shown to **enhance motor outcomes** by facilitating coordinated activity across different levels of the spinal cord.

This **interlimb interaction**, or _multisegmental facilitation_, can strengthen the functional link between arm and leg movements — an effect particularly relevant for tasks involving rhythmic coordination such as **walking, cycling, or stair climbing**. Such findings reinforce the concept that spinal cord stimulation can act not only locally but also as a **system-level modulator** of motor control.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">In Summary</span>

To summarize, **tSCS represents a powerful, non-invasive approach** for neuromodulation of spinal circuits. Its flexibility and safety make it a valuable tool for both research and rehabilitation. However, challenges remain in **optimizing electrode placement**, **improving patient comfort**, and **standardizing hardware reporting** to ensure reproducibility across studies. The recent expansion of tSCS applications toward **cervical stimulation** and **spinal network coupling** demonstrates the growing potential of this technology — not only for gait restoration but also for the **recovery of upper-limb function** and the **re-establishment of integrated motor control** across the entire spinal system.


## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Target Populations for Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)</span>

![[Pasted image 20251112202613.png]]

Spinal cord stimulation has been mainly studied in **spinal cord injury (SCI) patients**, as they represent the population with the most profound and direct disruption of spinal pathways. However, ongoing research is also exploring its use in **post-stroke rehabilitation** and in the **management of spasticity**, expanding its relevance far beyond mobility recovery alone

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) Recovery</span>

A **spinal cord injury** involves damage to the spinal tissue due to trauma, disease, or degenerative processes, leading to **partial or total loss of motor and autonomic functions**. This loss severely impacts patients’ independence, mobility, and quality of life.

Spinal cord stimulation—especially in its **transcutaneous form (tSCS)**—has emerged as a **rehabilitation tool** capable of modulating the **functional status of spinal networks below the lesion**. By increasing spinal excitability and improving the interaction between **residual cortical signals and spinal circuits**, tSCS enhances **voluntary motor responses** even in individuals previously unable to move certain muscles.

When used **alone or in combination with activity-based rehabilitation programs**, tSCS can lead to measurable improvements such as:

- Enhanced control of **upper and lower limbs**,
- Increased **trunk stability**, and
- Overall improvement in **quality of life**.
    
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Lower- and Upper-Limb Rehabilitation</span>

In **lower-limb rehabilitation**, **lumbar SCS** helps restore walking ability and voluntary muscle control. It works by engaging spinal **central pattern generators (CPGs)**—neural networks responsible for rhythmic motor activities such as gait. Patients often train in **safer setups**, like passive or assisted cycling, to eliminate the risk of falls while still stimulating the locomotor circuitry. This approach provides not only neurological but also **cardiovascular and musculoskeletal benefits**.

In contrast, **cervical SCS** targets **upper-limb function**, focusing on restoring fine motor control in the arms and hands. When paired with **movement-based therapy**, cervical SCS has been shown to:

- Increase **arm strength** and **dexterity**,
- Improve **joint mobility** and **cardiovascular responses**, and
- Sustain these gains for **months after the end of treatment**, indicating durable neuroplastic effects.
    

Such results have been reported in multiple studies (Inanici et al., Wagner et al., Gerasimenko et al.), demonstrating the potential of SCS to promote **functional recovery** even years after the initial injury.


### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Spasticity Control</span>

One of the most critical issues in SCI patients is **spasticity**, defined as **abnormal muscle tightness caused by prolonged involuntary contractions**. This condition arises from **reduced inhibitory control** and **hyperexcitability of spinal motor neurons** due to disrupted descending pathways. Spasticity not only limits movement but also causes pain, stiffness, and joint immobility, significantly affecting daily life.

Traditionally, spasticity is treated using **oral medications** or **botulinum toxin injections**, the latter being particularly effective. However, botulinum toxin has a **time-limited effect**—typically lasting around three months—and cannot be used too frequently due to habituation.

Spinal cord stimulation provides an **alternative or complementary approach**. By **modifying the excitability of lumbar spinal circuits** through continuous activation of posterior roots, SCS can **reduce spasticity** and facilitate more **coordinated muscle activation patterns**. Patients treated with SCS often show **improved walking speed** and **enhanced voluntary motor control**, making this one of the most impactful secondary benefits of the technique.

---

### Beyond SCI: Post-Stroke Rehabilitation

A particularly promising application of **tSCS** involves **stroke survivors**. In these patients, the spinal cord is structurally intact, but **descending motor commands from the brain are impaired** due to localized brain lesions. This means that the spinal circuitry remains functional but **underutilized**.

By **amplifying residual spinal pathways**, tSCS can facilitate **motor recovery** and improve both **upper- and lower-limb function**. Because stroke rehabilitation typically occurs over defined programs (six to nine months of therapy), **non-invasive transcutaneous stimulation** is preferred over implanted systems.

Recent studies have shown that:

- **Lumbar tSCS** can enhance **gait and lower-limb control**, while
- **Cervical tSCS** improves **arm and hand function**, **reduces spasticity**, and even enables movements—such as **hand opening**—that were lost for years.
    
The key challenge in stroke rehabilitation is to **synchronize stimulation with active exercise**, promoting **Hebbian plasticity**, where simultaneous activation of central and peripheral inputs strengthens neural connections. Thus, the goal is not merely to stimulate the spinal cord, but to **coordinate stimulation timing** with voluntary effort, leading to more meaningful and lasting motor recovery.


### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Summary</span>

In summary, spinal cord stimulation offers multiple pathways to improve function across diverse populations:

|**Population**|**Goal of SCS/tSCS**|**Key Effects**|
|---|---|---|
|**Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)**|Reactivate spinal networks below the lesion|Restored voluntary control, trunk stability, improved mobility|
|**SCI with Spasticity**|Normalize spinal excitability|Reduced muscle stiffness, better joint mobility|
|**Post-Stroke Patients**|Amplify residual spinal circuits|Improved gait, arm strength, hand dexterity|
|**Upper- vs Lower-Limb Focus**|Lumbar vs cervical stimulation|Targeted rehabilitation for specific motor functions|

# <span style="color:rgb(223, 109, 109)">Emerging Applications and Research Directions in Electrical Stimulation</span>


![[Pasted image 20251112210635.png]]
In recent years, the scope of **electrical stimulation for motor and sensory rehabilitation** has expanded dramatically, encompassing both **central** and **peripheral** targets, and integrating technologies such as **brain–computer interfaces (BCI)** and **functional electrical stimulation (FES)**. What was once limited to laboratory experiments has now evolved into **clinically meaningful interventions** and even **commercial prototypes**, reflecting both robustness in scientific evidence and translational progress.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Brain–Computer Interfaces and FES Integration</span>

A particularly innovative approach involves the **integration of BCI and FES systems**. The fundamental concept is to **link brain activity directly to muscle activation**: neural signals recorded from the brain (e.g., via EEG) are used to **command or modulate FES activation patterns** on the periphery.

This approach effectively **closes the loop** between intention and action, allowing users—particularly those with spinal cord or motor impairments—to reestablish a functional connection between the brain and the body. By pairing cortical activity with peripheral stimulation, this system encourages **neuroplastic reorganization**, promoting recovery of voluntary motor control even when traditional neural pathways are disrupted.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Tremor Suppression in Parkinson’s Disease</span>

Another exciting clinical application is **the use of spinal or peripheral electrical stimulation to suppress tremor** in patients with **Parkinson’s disease**. Studies have shown that **stimulating the forearm at specific frequencies** can significantly reduce pathological tremor amplitude.

Even more interesting is the **carryover effect** observed in many cases: after repeated stimulation sessions, patients not only experience tremor suppression during stimulation but also show **lasting improvements in motor control** afterward.

This indicates that the intervention does not merely act symptomatically, but may contribute to **long-term modulation of abnormal motor circuits**. As a result, this line of research has moved beyond the laboratory—several **start-ups in the United States** are already developing wearable stimulation devices, some of which are approaching commercialization.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Paired Corticomotor and Neural Stimulation</span>

At the **Shirley Ryan AbilityLab** in Chicago—formerly the Chicago Rehabilitation Institute—a significant line of research led by **Monica Perez** and **José Pons** explores **paired corticomotor–spinal stimulation**. This approach synchronizes **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** with **peripheral or spinal electrical stimulation**, aiming to **induce Hebbian plasticity** through precise timing between central and peripheral inputs.

By carefully coordinating these stimuli, researchers seek to **strengthen functional connectivity** between cortical and spinal circuits, enhancing recovery after injury. Monica Perez’s group is currently conducting **randomized controlled trials** to validate the clinical efficacy of this paired stimulation method, and the initial results are highly promising.

The **Shirley Ryan AbilityLab** itself represents an ideal environment for this kind of translational research—it is a **23-floor rehabilitation and research center**, where each floor is dedicated to a specific functional domain (e.g., upper limb, lower limb, speech, pediatric rehabilitation), and **research labs are physically integrated with clinical units**. This architecture encourages constant interaction between scientists and clinicians, accelerating the path from experimentation to therapy.


### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Electrical Stimulation, Proprioception, and Sense of Agency</span>

In another direction, **Andre Freiman** has investigated how **electrical stimulation can enhance proprioception**—the brain’s awareness of body position—and thus improve the **sense of agency**. His work focuses on **hand position perception**, demonstrating that applying targeted electrical stimulation can **improve the accuracy with which subjects perceive and control their hand position**.

This is particularly relevant for patients with motor impairments: if a person cannot reliably sense where their limb is or what it is doing, their ability to control it voluntarily is severely compromised. Enhancing proprioceptive feedback through stimulation therefore strengthens both **motor performance** and the **psychological sense of control** over movement.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Neuroprosthetics and Sensory Feedback Restoration</span>

A major challenge for **amputee patients** using advanced prosthetic limbs is the **lack of natural sensory feedback**. Without the ability to feel what the prosthesis is doing, tasks like grasping or manipulating objects become purely visual, increasing cognitive load and reducing control precision.

Research teams—especially at institutions such as **EPFL** and **ETH Zurich**—have been pioneering the implantation of **electrodes into peripheral nerves**, allowing artificial sensors on the prosthesis to send feedback signals directly to the user’s nervous system.

This bidirectional communication enables the user to **differentiate between textures, hardness, and grip strength**, achieving a much more natural interaction with the prosthetic limb. It also contributes to a stronger **sense of embodiment**—the perception that the prosthesis truly becomes part of the body.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Spinal and Peripheral Stimulation: Expanding Functional Targets</span>

Finally, **spinal cord and peripheral stimulation** are being explored for a wide range of physiological functions. For instance, **Marco Capogrosso**, a former student of **Silvestro Micera** now at the University of Pittsburgh, has advanced **cervical spinal stimulation** for motor recovery. Meanwhile, other leaders such as **Reggie Edgerton** (University of Louisville), **Grégoire Courtine** (EPFL), and **Silvestro Micera** (now at San Raffaele) have demonstrated that **epidural stimulation** can be applied not only for locomotion but also for **autonomic functions**, including **respiration, cardiovascular control, bladder function**, and even **intestinal activity**.

Interestingly, some of these effects were observed **unexpectedly**. For example, in early trials conducted on **pilots participating in the Cybathlon**, spinal stimulation did not significantly improve muscle power output or performance. However, one participant reported a **remarkable improvement in intestinal function**, a major concern for spinal cord injury patients. This highlights how **secondary or unanticipated benefits** of stimulation can still have profound impacts on quality of life.
