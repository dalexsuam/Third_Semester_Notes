## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Digital Temperature Sensor</span>

Today we are going to talk about the **temperature sensor on the board**. Unlike the internal temperature sensor inside the microcontroller, which is **analog** and therefore requires conversion through the **ADC**, this external temperature sensor is **digital**.  

Because it is digital, **no ADC conversion is required**. Instead, the sensor communicates by sending already-converted digital data directly to the microcontroller.
![[Pasted image 20251123081557.png|500]]
The image above shows the **block diagram** of the sensor. We will examine its internal structure later, but for now there is something important to notice: at the bottom of the diagram, the sensor exposes two pins labeled **SCL** and **SDA**.

These two signals are new compared to what we used before. They belong to the **I²C interface**, which is a communication protocol designed for connecting digital sensors and peripherals to a microcontroller.

- **SCL** stands for _Serial Clock_. It is the clock line generated by the master (the microcontroller).
- **SDA** stands for _Serial Data_. It is the data line where the bits are transmitted.
    
These two wires are characteristic of any I²C device. Digital sensors that use I²C, like this temperature sensor, rely on these lines to exchange digital information with the MCU. In the next sections, we will explain how I²C works and how to use it to read temperature data from this sensor.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">I²C: Inter-Integrated Circuit (IIC → I²C)</span>

I²C, short for _Inter-Integrated Circuit_ (originally written IIC), is a communication protocol designed to allow many devices to share the same two wires. Unlike UART, which is strictly point-to-point and only connects one transmitter with one receiver, I²C allows several sensors and controllers to coexist on a single bus using only three connections: SCL (clock), SDA (data), and a shared ground. This makes it extremely practical for systems where multiple peripherals need to communicate with a microcontroller without using many pins.

One of the main differences from UART is that I²C is not limited to a single master–single slave relationship. The same two wires can be used by multiple masters and multiple slaves. This means that a microcontroller can initiate communication, but in some designs even a sensor can act as a master. All devices listen on the same bus, and communication begins only when a master addresses a specific slave. This flexibility is what makes I²C suitable for complex embedded systems.

Regarding speed, I²C offers several operating modes: Standard Mode at 100 kbit/s and Fast Mode at 400 kbit/s, both supported by our microcontroller. Other MCUs may offer faster modes such as Fast Mode+ (1 Mbit/s) or High-Speed Mode (3.4 Mbit/s), but these are not available on our board. Increasing speed increases throughput but also makes timing requirements more demanding.

Both SCL and SDA are bidirectional lines. This is important because many sensors contain internal registers for configuration, status reporting, and data output. A typical interaction begins with the master writing configuration values to the sensor, followed by reading back data. As sensors become more complex, the number of required register transactions increases, and I²C provides a simple way to manage them.
![[Pasted image 20251123082631.png|400]]

Electrically, I²C relies on pull-up resistors connected to both SCL and SDA. The lines idle in the HIGH state, and devices communicate by pulling them LOW. This is known as an open-drain (or open-collector) architecture. Pulling the line down is active: a transistor drives the line to ground, allowing a fast falling edge. However, returning the line to HIGH is passive: the pull-up resistor must charge the parasitic capacitances of the bus, meaning the rising edge is slower. The speed of this rising transition depends on the resistor value, the total bus capacitance, the wiring length, and the number of devices connected. As a result, adding more sensors increases the total capacitance and slows the rising edge even more.

This design choice—active pull-down and passive pull-up—is the fundamental reason why I²C cannot reach very high speeds compared to other protocols such as SPI. SPI uses push-pull drivers that actively drive both HIGH and LOW, achieving much faster transitions.

In summary, I²C trades raw speed for simplicity and versatility. It only requires two wires, supports many devices on the same bus, allows both reading and writing through bidirectional lines, and offers a clear structure for multi-master and multi-slave communication. Although the open-drain architecture limits its maximum speed, it remains an ideal solution for most sensors and peripherals commonly used in embedded systems.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">I2C - Start & Stop<br></span>
As mentioned earlier, when no transmission is occurring, both the SCL and SDA lines remain in the high state due to the pull-up resistors. However, for a communication system with multiple masters and multiple slaves to function correctly, all devices must share a common understanding of **when a transmission begins** and **when it ends**. This is why the I²C protocol defines two special conditions: the **START condition** and the **STOP condition**.
![[Pasted image 20251123083745.png|400]]
The clock line (SCL) simply continues to toggle between high and low during normal operation. The crucial signaling occurs on the data line (SDA). A START condition is recognized when **SDA transitions from high to low while SCL is high**. This specific transition is universally interpreted by all devices on the bus—masters and slaves—as the beginning of a new communication frame.

This shared mechanism is essential. For example, when the microcontroller (acting as a master) wants to communicate with a sensor, it generates a START condition. All other devices connected to the bus observe this and immediately understand that the bus is now in use. They must remain silent and avoid driving the lines until the ongoing communication is complete. In other words, the START condition signals: _“The bus is now busy; do not interfere.”_

At the end of a transmission, the master generates a STOP condition. This occurs when **SDA transitions from low to high while SCL is high**. When all devices detect this event, they understand that the communication has ended and that the bus is free again. They can now attempt to initiate their own transmissions if needed.

This mechanism functions much like a shared classroom rule. When one student raises their hand (the START condition), everyone else understands that this student has the right to speak, and they wait. When the student finishes speaking (the STOP condition), everyone recognizes that the floor is open again.

These START and STOP conditions ensure orderly communication on a shared bus, preventing collisions and guaranteeing that every device knows exactly when a message begins and ends.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Addressing </span>

![[Pasted image 20251123100752.png]]
When the start bit appears on the I²C bus, it indicates that a complete data packet is about to be transmitted. After the start condition, the first information placed on the bus is always the device address. Most devices, including those on our microcontroller, use the standard 7-bit addressing format. These seven bits identify a specific device among all the peripherals that share the same SDA and SCL lines. The idea is simple: if several sensors are connected, the microcontroller must specify which one it wants to communicate with—almost like calling the device by name. If the microcontroller wants data from the temperature sensor, it sends the temperature sensor’s address; if it wants information from the accelerometer, it sends the accelerometer’s address instead.

Following the 7-bit address, there is one additional bit called the R/W bit. This final bit tells the addressed device what kind of operation the master intends to perform. A value of 0 corresponds to a write operation, meaning that the master wants to send data to the sensor. A value of 1 corresponds to a read operation, meaning that the master is requesting data from the sensor. In this way, the combination of the address and the R/W bit fully specifies both _who_ is being targeted and _what_ action is expected.

Once the address and the R/W bit are sent, the protocol requires an acknowledgement bit (ACK). This acknowledgement is essential. The slave device must pull the line low to confirm that it has correctly received the address and is ready to proceed with the communication. If this acknowledgement does not arrive, the master interprets this as an error. It may mean that the addressed sensor is not present on the bus, that the address is incorrect, or that the data on the line was corrupted. In any case, the absence of an ACK stops the operation, and the master will typically report an error state. This is precisely why, in our HAL functions, the return value HAL_OK is important: it indicates that the acknowledgement was received and that the transaction is proceeding as expected. If we accidentally attempt to communicate with a device at the wrong address, no device will acknowledge the message and the function will return an error.
![[Pasted image 20251123100852.png]]
Although the standard format uses seven address bits, I²C also supports a 10-bit addressing mode for more complex systems with a large number of devices. However, this expanded addressing scheme is not required for the sensors we are working with, so we will focus on the simpler and more commonly used 7-bit format.
#### <span style="color:rgb(2, 141, 192)">Summarizing</span>

In every I²C exchange, the first thing the master sends is always the address of the device it wants to communicate with. This is the step in which the microcontroller “calls” the sensor by name to make sure that only the intended device reacts. Immediately after the address, the microcontroller also sends the R/W bit, which specifies whether the next part of the communication will be a write operation or a read operation. For this reason, every I²C communication begins with a short transmission from the master to the slave, even in the case where the master ultimately wants to receive data. The master must always introduce the communication by identifying the device and declaring the direction of data flow.

After this initial phase, the rest of the exchange depends on the selected operation. If the master intends to write, the next bytes it sends will contain either the register address inside the sensor, the actual data to be written, or both. If the master intends to read, then after specifying the device address and the read request, the sensor becomes the transmitter and sends the requested data back to the master, which receives it and stores it.

This makes I²C a multi-step protocol:

1. **Step 1:** The master sends the device address and the read/write bit.
2. **Step 2:** Depending on that bit, the communication continues either with additional bytes sent by the master (write) or by the slave (read).
    
In practice, this means that even when the master only wants to read data, the transaction always begins with the master sending information first. The communication then proceeds with the appropriate transmit or receive phase, making each I²C transfer a structured sequence rather than a single exchange.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Data Packets and Speed</span>

In I²C, all data is transmitted in **bytes**, and every byte follows the same structure: a start condition, an 8-bit data package, and an acknowledgement bit. The acknowledgement is essential for controlling the flow of communication. If the expected acknowledgement is not received, the transmission is immediately interrupted.

There are two situations to distinguish regarding acknowledgements:

1. **Slave Receiver Mode**  
    When the slave is receiving data (meaning the master is writing), the slave **must** acknowledge every byte. If the acknowledgement does not arrive, the master interprets this as an error and the communication stops.
2. **Master Receiver Mode**  
    When the master is receiving data from the slave (meaning the slave is sending), the master can deliberately send a **NACK** after the last byte. This NACK informs the slave that no further bytes are needed, and therefore terminates the communication. In this case the NACK is intentional and is used to close the transmission cleanly.
    
![[Pasted image 20251123101618.png|500]]

Regarding the electrical behavior of the bus, the transition from HIGH to LOW is very fast. This is because pulling the line low is done actively by transistors, and the bus must react immediately to prevent simultaneous transmissions from different devices. The opposite transition (LOW to HIGH) is slower, because it relies on the pull-up resistor and the capacitive elements of the bus. The resulting waveform follows an RC charging curve governed by:

- the value of the pull-up resistor (typically between 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ)
- the total parasitic capacitance of the bus and the devices (typically 10 to 100 pF)

These two parameters determine the time constant of the line and the speed at which the signal rises back to the HIGH state.

If the pull-up resistor is **too small**, the rising edge becomes too steep, leading to instability or to the voltage never reaching a clear logic HIGH level. Digital communication relies on defined thresholds, and if the signal never crosses the threshold, the line may be interpreted as always LOW.

If the pull-up resistor is **too large**, the rising edge becomes too slow. In this case, the signal might not reach the HIGH level within the time required by the I²C clock frequency, causing communication errors.

For this reason, the pull-up value must be chosen carefully—not too low and not too high—so that the rise time matches the speed required by the I²C bus.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Transmission Example</span>

![[Pasted image 20251123101743.png|500]]
This diagram simply illustrates the general structure of an I²C data transfer. Every communication begins with a **start condition**, followed by the **7-bit address** of the target device and the **read/write bit**, which specifies whether the master wants to read from or write to that device. After this, the receiving device must send an **acknowledgement bit**, confirming that the address and the read/write request were correctly received.

Once the address phase is completed, the actual data transfer begins. Depending on the direction selected (read or write), either the master sends data to the slave or the slave sends data to the master. Regardless of the direction, **each byte sent must be followed by an acknowledgement bit**. This acknowledgement is what allows the communication to continue; without it, the transmission stops immediately.

When all required bytes have been exchanged, the master issues the **stop condition**, which signals that the communication is complete and releases the bus for other devices.
 
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">HAL functions for I2C</span>

The functions used for I²C communication are straightforward because the microcontroller can only perform two basic operations: **transmit** data to a device or **receive** data from a device. To do this, we use one of the I²C peripherals available on the microcontroller.

~~~c#
HAL_StatusTypeDef HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit(I2C_HandleTypeDef *hi2c, uint16_t DevAddress, uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size, uint32_t Timeout) 

HAL_StatusTypeDef HAL_I2C_Master_Receive(I2C_HandleTypeDef *hi2c, uint16_t DevAddress, uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size, uint32_t Timeout)
~~~

Each transmit or receive function requires a few essential parameters. The first one is the **device address**, which identifies the specific sensor or peripheral we want to communicate with. This address is always found in the device’s datasheet. Traditionally, the last bit of the address must be set to **0 for a write operation** or **1 for a read operation**. Some recent versions of STM32CubeIDE handle this automatically, but explicitly setting the bit is still good practice because it removes ambiguity.

Next, for a **transmit operation**, we provide a pointer to the **data we want to send**, the **size** of that data, and a **timeout**. The timeout defines how long the microcontroller will wait for the communication to succeed; if the operation takes too long or the slave does not acknowledge, the function stops and returns an error status.

For a **receive operation**, the structure is identical, but instead of sending data, we provide a pointer to the **buffer where the received data should be stored**.

The same type of API functions also exist for interrupt-based transfers and DMA-based transfers, but the general structure and required parameters remain the same.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">Digital Temperature Sensor LM75/LM75B</span>

The sensor we are using is a **bandgap digital temperature sensor**. Depending on the board version, there may be two possible versions of this sensor: one with **9-bit resolution** and another with **11-bit resolution**. The resolution affects the **least significant bit (LSB) value**, which determines how precise each measurement is. The sensor internally completes the analog-to-digital conversion, so we don’t have to handle that ourselves.

The sensor uses **two’s complement format** to represent temperature, which allows it to represent both positive and negative values. The data is split into two parts:
![[Pasted image 20251123103102.png]]

- The **first byte** (8 bits) contains the **integer part** of the temperature.
- The **second byte** contains the **fractional part**, which can be 1 bit or 3 bits depending on the sensor version.
    
The sensor has a **temperature register** at address 0, which stores the current temperature value. We read this register via the **I²C interface**.

![[Pasted image 20251123103154.png]]

Each I²C device has an **address**. In this case, the base address of the sensor is `1001xxx`. The last three bits (`xxx`) can be set via pins on the PCB, either to **high (1)** or **low (0)**. This allows us to connect up to **8 identical sensors** on the same I²C bus, each with a unique address. Without this, multiple sensors would respond simultaneously to the same command, creating conflicts.

![[Pasted image 20251123081557.png|500]]
Internally, the sensor contains a **Sigma-Delta ADC**, either 9-bit or 11-bit depending on the version. This makes the sensor “smart,” as it handles conversion internally. It also provides multiple **registers** beyond the temperature reading, which the microcontroller can access. For example:

- The **configuration register** allows the microcontroller to set sensor parameters.    
- The **TOS register** sets temperature thresholds. The sensor can monitor the temperature on its own and raise an interrupt (via an EXTI line) if a threshold is exceeded. This allows the microcontroller to react only when needed, without continuously polling the sensor.
- Additional registers allow further configuration or status checking.
    
Even though this sensor is relatively simple, it demonstrates how modern digital sensors can perform internal processing and provide configurable features, not just raw data readings. This is a shift from analog sensors toward **digital, smart sensors** that offload some tasks from the microcontroller.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Let's delve a bit into the Datasheet</span>

![[Pasted image 20251123103354.png|400]]

![[Pasted image 20251123103415.png|400]]

When working with a digital temperature sensor, the **datasheet** is an essential reference. It provides detailed information about the sensor, including a **block diagram**, **pinout**, and descriptions of the registers and configuration options. For instance, you can see how the sensor is physically connected to the PCB and how its pins (like A2, A1, A0) are used to define the device address. In our case, we only use **one temperature sensor**, so all three address pins are set to ground (`0, 0, 0`), which matches the address used in our slides.


The datasheet also includes **descriptions of each register** inside the sensor:
![[Pasted image 20251123103502.png|400]]

- **Configuration register**: Can be **read and written**. This allows the microcontroller to configure the sensor’s behavior and check if the configuration is correct.
- **Temperature register**: **Read-only**, since the sensor itself writes the current temperature. The microcontroller only reads from this register.
- **Over-temperature shutdown threshold (TOS)** and **hysteresis register**: Optional registers for advanced control, which we are not using in this project.
    
![[Pasted image 20251123103620.png|400]]

The sensor also includes a **pointer register**. This register indicates **which internal register** the sensor will read from or write to. It essentially acts as a “selector” so that when we communicate with the sensor, we tell it:

1. Which **device** we want to talk to (using the I²C device address).
2. Which **register** inside the device we want to access (using the pointer register).
3. Whether we want to **read or write** data.
    
The sequence of communication typically looks like this:

![[Pasted image 20251123103818.png|500]]

1. The microcontroller sends a **start condition**.
2. It transmits the **device address** along with the **read/write bit**.
3. It specifies the **register address** using the pointer register.
4. The data is then **written to or read from** the selected register.
5. The communication ends with a **stop condition**.

Even though the sensor has only four registers in this example, this structure allows for clear and precise communication. The pointer register ensures that each read or write operation targets the correct register in the sensor’s memory.

In summary, understanding the datasheet helps you see **how to configure the sensor, which registers are available, and how to read or write data properly**. Even if we don’t use all registers, knowing their purpose is important for more advanced projects.

# <span style="color:rgb(223, 109, 109)">Project 7a: Read the temperature measured by the LM75 and send it to a remote terminal every 1 second. </span>
The goal of this project is to **read the temperature measured by the LM75 sensor** and send it over UART every second. For simplicity, we read only the **most significant byte (MSB, 8 bits)** of the temperature register.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">1. Hardware Setup</span>
![[Pasted image 20251027195344.png|500]]
- The LM75 sensor communicates via **I²C**.
- The **I²C1 interface** on the microcontroller uses the following pins:
    - **SCL → PB8**
    - **SDA → PB9**
- Each device on the I²C bus has a unique **7-bit address**. The LM75 also has a **pointer register** that selects which internal register we want to access.
As a first step we will read only the MSB 8 bit.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">2. Define Addresses</span>
~~~c#
uint8_t LM75_ADDRESS = 0b10010000; //Address left-shifted by 1, 0 padded
uint8_t LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS= 0x00; // Address of pointer register
~~~
**Explanation:**
1. `LM75_ADDRESS` is the **device address** of the LM75 sensor.
    - The last bit of the 8-bit transmission determines **Read (1)** or **Write (0)**.
    - Initially, we set it to **0** (write) to configure the pointer register.
    - Later, we add `+1` when reading (`Read = 1`).

![[Pasted image 20251027193542.png|500]]
1. `LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS` is the **internal register address**, stored in the pointer register.
    - This tells the sensor **where to read the data from** next.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">3. Initialize the Sensor (Set Pointer Register)</span>

Before reading the temperature, we must tell the LM75 which register we want to access:
~~~c#
HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit(&hi2c1, LM75_ADDRESS, &LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS, 1, 10);
~~~
- **Purpose:** Write the pointer register so the sensor knows we want to read the temperature.
- `LM75_ADDRESS` → I²C device address (write mode, last bit = 0)
- `&LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS` → Value of the pointer register (temperature register)
- `1` → Number of bytes transmitted
- `10` → Timeout in milliseconds

> After this step, the LM75 is “pointing” to its temperature register.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">4. Main Loop: Read Temperature</span>
```c#
while(1){
	uint8_t temperature = 0; // we want to read only 1 byte -> 8 bits
	int len = 0;
	char str[32];
	if(HAL_I2C_Master_Receive(&hi2c1, LM75_ADDRESS+1, &temperature, 1, 20)== HAL_OK){ 
	// we check if the communication went right before we send the data
	//Note the LM75_ADDRESS+1, the last bit is to read/write, 0->write, 1->read, thus +1, we want to read 
	//Instead of indicating where to write, we add where to save the read data &temperature
		char str[32];
		len = snprintf(str, sizeof(str), "Temperature: %d °C \r\n", temperature);
		} else{
		
			len = snprintf(str, sizeof(str), "Error reading from LM75 \r\n");
		}
		
		HAL_UART_Transmit(&huart2, str, len, 100);
		HAL_Delay(1000);
}
```
**Explanation of the main loop:**
1. **Receive data from the sensor:**
    - `LM75_ADDRESS + 1` sets the **read bit**.
    - `&temperature` → variable to store the read data.
    - `1` → read **1 byte**, the MSB of the temperature.
    - `20` → timeout in milliseconds.
2. **Check the HAL status:**
    - `HAL_OK` confirms that the communication (start, address, ACK, data, stop) succeeded.
    - Otherwise, an error message is prepared.
3. **Send temperature via UART:**
    - `snprintf` formats the message.
    - `HAL_UART_Transmit` sends the string to a remote terminal.
4. **Delay:**
    - `HAL_Delay(1000)` ensures the data is sent **every 1 second**.
    - Note: HAL_Delay is blocking and generally not used in production code or advanced projects, but it’s fine here for simplicity.

### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">2. Code Implementation</span>
 ***Address Definitions***
```c#
uint8_t LM75_ADDRESS = 0b10010000;
uint8_t LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS = 0x00;

int8_t temperature_buffer[6];
int16_t temperature = 0;

char string[32];
int length = 0;
```
- `LM75_ADDRESS`: This is the base address of the sensor, left-shifted to leave the LSB for read/write.
- `LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS`: The pointer register in the sensor that holds the temperature data.

***Buffers and Variables***

```c#
uint8_t temperature_buffer[6];
int16_t temperature = 0;

char string[32];
int length = 0;
```
- `temperature_buffer[6]`: Temporary storage for multiple bytes read via DMA.
- `temperature`: Stores the combined 16-bit temperature value.
- `string` and `length`: Used for UART transmission to send the temperature as a string.
    

> **Note:** Using either `int16_t` or `uint16_t`ensures enough bits to accommodate the extended 11-bit data.

***Initialization***
~~~c#
//Main init
HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit(&hi2c1, LM75_ADDRESS, &LM75_TEMP_ADDRESS, 1, 100);

HAL_TIM_Base_Start_IT(&htim2);
~~~
- The first line sets the **pointer register** of the LM75 to the temperature register.
- `HAL_TIM_Base_Start_IT(&htim2)`: Starts TIM2 in interrupt mode to trigger temperature reads at regular intervals.

***Timer Interrupt Callback***
``` c#

void HAL_TIM_PeriodElapsedCallback(TIM_HandleTypeDef *htim){
	if(htim == &htim2){
		HAL_I2C_Master_Receive_DMA(&hi2c1, LM75_ADDRESS+1, temperature_buffer, 6);
	}
}
```
- Every time TIM2 triggers an interrupt, the microcontroller **initiates a DMA-based I2C read**.
- `LM75_ADDRESS+1` indicates a **read operation**.
- `temperature_buffer` stores the incoming data (up to 6 bytes for redundancy/verification).

***I2C Receive Complete Callback***
```c#
void HAL_I2C_MasterRxCpltCallback(I2C_HandleTypeDef *hi2c){
	if(hi2c == &hi2c1){
		if(temperature_buffer[0]==temperature_buffer[2] || temperature_buffer[0] == temperature_buffer[4]){

			temperature = temperature_buffer[0]<<8 | temperature_buffer[1];
			}
		else{
			temperature = temperature_buffer[2]<<8 | temperature_buffer[3];
			}
//
// //for +25.375
// temperature_buffer[0] = 0b00011001; // 0x19
// temperature_buffer[1] = 0b01100000; // 0x60
// //for −12.875
// temperature_buffer[0] = 0b11110011; // 0xF3
// temperature_buffer[1] = 0b00010000; // 0x10

		temperature = temperature_buffer[0]<<8 | temperature_buffer[1];
		float temperature_celsius = temperature/256.0;
		length = snprintf(string, sizeof(string), "Temperature: %.3f °C \r \n", temperature_celsius);
		HAL_UART_Transmit_DMA(&huart2, string, length);

	}
}
```

- Once the DMA read is complete, this callback is executed.
- **Redundancy check**: The LM75 can return multiple copies of the MSB for reliability. We check `temperature_buffer[0]` against `temperature_buffer[2]` or `[4]` to pick a valid reading.
- Combine the two bytes into a **16-bit value**: `temperature_buffer[0]<<8 | temperature_buffer[1]`.
- Convert to Celsius: divide by 256 (`temperature/256.0`).
- Format as a string and transmit via **UART DMA**.

## <span style="color:rgb(239, 179, 1)">HAL<i>I2C</i>Mem<i>Read</i>DMA Function</span>

This function allows reading data from an I2C device using DMA. It simplifies the process by combining the memory address transmission and data reception in a single function.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Function Prototype</span>
```c
HAL_I2C_Mem_Read_DMA(I2C_HandleTypeDef *hi2c, uint16_t DevAddress, 
                     uint16_t MemAddress, uint16_t MemAddSize, 
                     uint8_t *pData, uint16_t Size);
```

#### <span style="color:rgb(2, 141, 192)">Parameters Explained</span>

1. *_I2C_HandleTypeDef _hi2c → &hi2c1__
    - Pointer to the I2C handle.
    - Identifies which I2C peripheral is being used.
2. **uint16_t DevAddress → LM75B_ADDR**
    - The 7-bit I2C address of the LM75B sensor, extended to 8 bits.
    - Initialized as `(0b1001000 << 1)`.
3. **uint16_t MemAddress → TEMP_REG_ADDR**
    - The internal register address of the sensor to read from (temperature register).
    - Value: `0b00000000`.
4. **uint16_t MemAddSize → I2C_MEMADD_SIZE_8BIT**
    - Specifies that the internal memory address size is 8 bits.
5. *_uint8_t _pData → therm_i2c_rx__
    - Pointer to a buffer of size 2 to store the data read from the sensor.
6. **uint16_t Size → 2**
    - Number of bytes to read from the sensor (2 bytes in this example).
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Key Notes</span>

- **MemRead vs MemRead DMA vs MemRead Interrupt:**
    - `HAL_I2C_Mem_Read_DMA` is convenient because it handles both sending the internal register address and receiving the data automatically.
    - `HAL_I2C_Mem_Read` (non-DMA) or interrupt versions require separate steps for address transmission and data reception.
- **Efficiency:**
    - Some say `HAL_I2C_Mem_Read_DMA` is slower due to internal overhead, but for simple reads (like 2 bytes) this is negligible.
- **Temperature Reading Considerations:**
    - If the buffer is defined as `int8_t`, negative values can appear incorrect.
    - Conversion between types (e.g., `int8_t` → `int16_t`) may extend bits differently depending on whether the type is signed or unsigned.
    - Unsigned types: extra bits are filled with `0`.
    - Signed types: extra bits are filled to preserve the sign (two's complement).

#### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Example: Two's Complement Extension</span>
- Positive number 21:
    - `uint8_t 00010101 → uint16_t 0000000000010101`
    - `int8_t 00010101 → int16_t 0000000000010101`
- Negative number -21:
    - `int8_t 11101011 → int16_t 1111111111101011`
- The extra bits ensure the number remains correct in signed representation.
### <span style="color:rgb(161, 40, 226)">Summary</span>
- `HAL_I2C_Mem_Read_DMA` = automatic transmit (internal register) + receive (data) + DMA buffer handling.
- Simplifies reading from I2C sensors.
- Pay attention to signed vs unsigned conversions when reading multi-byte registers.
- Slight overhead compared to manual transmit/receive, but practical for most applications.
